# 2026-10-13 progress overview

## Pause rule on fresh sessions: it flagged nothing

**What was tested.** The "pause between rallies" rule (a rally is practice-like when it is followed AND preceded by a gap of 6 seconds or less) was the first rule in this investigation that removed practice at almost no cost, but only on the clips it was fitted on. Today it was run on 8 unseen PGC segments (68 detected rallies).

**What happened.** It flagged none of them, so the pre-registered check cannot be scored. The gap code itself is fine (on the fitted sessions it flags about 9% of rallies). You then judged 30 clips from these sessions: about half were real rallies, 2 were serve-and-return only, and 13 were not a rally. Only one of those 13 was practice, and 11 of those 13 came from one camera (court 1, which was also two thirds of the sample). So the rule had nothing to catch: the false alarms here are a different kind, mostly isolated events with long gaps around them.

**What it means for next steps.** Do not put the rule into ranking. The open problem is the false alarms at court 1: we do not yet know what they were. Next: your reasons for those clips, then a GPU-free check of whether fixed bright spots explain them. Nothing shipped, nothing changed in production.

**Also discussed, not decided.** Using a vision model (Gemini or a local one) to veto candidates after TrackNet: ball machine / drill vs. play / "is a real exchange present". First step would be reproducing the old encoding-sensitivity problem (ADR-085) on ~20 judged clips. Open: whether field-test footage may be sent to an API at all (consent) or a local model should be used.

## Gemini on its own vs. the pipeline: it fails

**What was tested.** Instead of TrackNet + RunPod, send two 15-minute brickwall excerpts straight to Gemini with a written definition of a rally, and score it exactly like the pipeline (same labels, same overlap rule). The bar was fixed before any call.

**What happened.** It lost. Pooled, Gemini got 59% precision / 52% recall against the pipeline's 73% / 67%. The deciding problem is repeatability: the same video re-encoded gave a completely different answer (agreement 0.15, needed 0.85), and in the bad runs it reported rallies at times past the end of the video. When it kept the right clock it was as good as or better than the pipeline (91% / 81% on one excerpt), so the weakness looks like time-keeping, not understanding.

**What it means.** TrackNet stays. Gemini is not a replacement, and not a safe filter in this form. One follow-up is worth its own test: show the model a clock burned into the picture and use short chunks. Nothing changed in production.

## Serve landing from the 3D flight tool: it does not work on these clips

**What was tested.** Whether the flight-tracing tool (built on 2026-10-11 for one rally) can say where a serve came down, well enough to tell a failed serve from a good one. 25 of the judged clips, rules and pass bar fixed before running.

**What happened.** It failed. It produced an answer for only 7 of 25 serves (needed 15), called 5 of the 6 good serves among them faults, and got the one real fault wrong. Four of the seven answers put the landing 10 to 40 ft outside the court, so the tool was guessing, not measuring.

**What it means.** We won't build a serve-fault detector on this tool. It doesn't prove landings are impossible, but we have no evidence they're recoverable. Remaining ideas, none started: zoom the detector in on the court, use the players' positions at the serve, mine more labelled faults, and the delivery-format change.

## Serve landing, the simple way: also fails, and we now know why

**What was tested.** Following your point that we only need where the serve first touches the ground: find that bounce in the picture and place it on the court using the calibration. Same 25 clips and the same pass bar as the flight-tool test.

**What happened.** It failed again. It found a landing for only 9 of 25 serves (needed 15) and called 5 of the 8 good serves among them faults. The reason is the same one behind the earlier failure: in the few seconds around a serve the ball is confirmed on only about a quarter of the frames (median 32 of 135), so a bounce is usually not caught.

**What it means.** Whether we can ever read a serve landing from this camera depends on seeing the ball more often. The next test is to zoom the detector in on the court and count how much more often the ball is seen. If that doesn't help, landing-based fault detection is off the table at 30 fps.

## Body points on the venue clips: the far side looks better than the near side

**What was measured.** Whether the pose model we already have gives usable body points (especially wrists, needed to see a swing) in the seconds around a serve, for each side of the court. 30 judged clips.

**What happened.** On the far side, both wrists had a confident reading for 91% of players. On the camera's side only 53% did, because players there stand at the baseline right next to the camera and a third of them are cut off by the bottom of the picture. That is the opposite of what I expected.

**What it does not tell us.** These are the model's own confidence scores. Nothing has been watched yet, so we don't know the points are in the right place, especially for far-side players who are only 134 pixels tall. And we have not yet checked whether a swing can be read from them.

**What's next.** Draw the body points on a few clips and have you judge in playback. Only if they look right do we try the "did someone swing after the serve" check.
